Narrative pitch

How I would sell, in four paragraphs

I don't sell models. I sell operating leverage. When I walk into a Fortune 500 account, the question I'm answering isn't "do you want AI?" — every executive in the building already knows the answer. The real question is "whose AI gets embedded into how your company actually runs in the next 24 months?" I want to be the partner that question gets answered with.

My selling motion starts with a deliberate land: a single high-signal department where I can prove measurable lift inside one quarter. Support deflection. Sales-rep ramp time. Legal contract turnaround. I'd rather win 200 seats with a publishable internal case study than 2,000 seats and a champion who can't defend the ROI to the CFO.

The expansion thesis is what makes this role different from any other enterprise sale. Once a function is using a frontier model daily, two things happen: usage expands sideways into adjacent functions, and the customer's product team starts asking what's possible through the API. The same logo can be a $400K seat deal in year one and a $10M API consumption account in year three — but only if I'm engineering for that arc from day one.

Ultimately, I'm hired to own a number, but I win by being the executive's most trusted explainer of a fast-moving technology. Curiosity is my unfair advantage. If I understand their roadmap better than their last vendor did, the renewal conversation never starts from zero.

Core thesis

AI is operating infrastructure, not a point solution

The category-defining mistake is treating frontier AI as an "IT tool" with a CIO-shaped buyer. It isn't. It's a horizontal substrate that every department touches — which means the TAM has no ceiling, because every function becomes a buyer.

My principles

Five things I refuse to do

  • Sell a feature the customer can't actually operationalize in 90 days.
  • Forecast a deal I haven't pressure-tested with the economic buyer directly.
  • Promise capability I can't ship — credibility compounds, so does the loss of it.
  • Treat procurement as an obstacle. They're a stakeholder; I sell to them too.
  • Confuse activity with progress. A meeting without a next-step decision didn't happen.

Tab 03 · Deal Strategy

How the deal actually closes.

Qualification, mutual action, competitive positioning, expansion signals, and the objections I should be able to answer in my sleep.

Qualification — MEDDPICC, adapted for frontier AI

Metrics

Specific, baselined, and tied to a function the customer already reports on. "30% reduction in Tier-1 ticket cost over two quarters" — not "improve productivity."

Economic Buyer

Person who can sign without committee. For seat deals it's typically a function head with discretionary budget; for API and platform deals it climbs to CTO/CFO/CEO.

Decision Criteria

What the customer says they're judging vs. what the org will actually weigh. Get both, in writing, before proposal.

Decision Process

Stages, gates, exec reviews, security review, procurement, board approval if >$X. Map the org chart against the timeline.

Paper Process

Procurement, legal, InfoSec, privacy review, MSAs and DPAs. Start in week one, not week ten — security review alone can be 6–10 weeks at large enterprises.

Identified Pain

Quantified, owned by a named person, and visible to leadership. If no one loses their bonus over it, the deal slips.

Champion

Internal seller with the political capital to defend the deal in a room I'm not in. Test them: have they fought for me when I wasn't on the call?

Competition

Not just other vendors — also "do nothing," "build internally," and "wait six months for the next model." All three can kill a deal.

Competitive positioning

vs. Microsoft Copilot

"Bundled, but generic"

Where they win: existing M365 footprint, single-vendor procurement, IT comfort.

Where I win: frontier-model quality, faster release cadence, multi-modal depth (Realtime, vision, agents), API-first surface for product embedding. Copilot is a productivity feature; I'm selling a platform.

How I frame it: "Copilot is a great mass-deployment story for Office workflows. For the workloads where model quality determines whether the project ships at all, you'll want us — and most of our customers run both."

vs. Google Gemini

"Strong models, fragmented motion"

Where they win: Workspace-native customers, very long context, price/perf in some tiers.

Where I win: developer ecosystem maturity, Realtime + voice, faster enterprise admin/audit features, deeper partner channel.

How I frame it: "Strong models — but the question is which ecosystem your engineers actually want to build on. Look at where the open-source tooling, evals, and recipes are."

vs. Anthropic Claude

"Capable peer, narrower surface"

Where they win: long-context reasoning, safety-conscious buyers, strong coding model.

Where I win: breadth of modalities (voice, vision, image gen), ChatGPT brand recognition that pulls users into the platform without training, faster agentic surface, more mature fine-tuning.

How I frame it: "Claude is a credible reasoning model; we don't disagree there. But you're not just buying one model — you're buying the platform around it: voice, agents, evals, fine-tuning, the brand your end-users already know."

vs. AWS Bedrock

"Marketplace, not a partner"

Where they win: existing AWS commit burn-down, model-shopping convenience, infrastructure proximity.

Where I win: first-party model access without abstraction layers, faster availability of new capabilities, direct relationship with the lab shipping the model — no telephone game when something breaks.

How I frame it: "Bedrock is fine if your priority is burning AWS commit. If your priority is being on the frontier the day it ships and having a phone number when production breaks, we're a different conversation."

Objection handling

01"We're worried about our data being used to train your models."

"Reasonable concern, and the answer is direct: Enterprise and API data are not used to train our models by default. Your data stays your data. I'll send you the relevant section of the DPA, but more importantly I'll set up a 30-minute call with our trust team — they'll walk through your specific data flows and what happens at each step. Most of our customers find that conversation more reassuring than any marketing page."

02"We already have Microsoft Copilot through our M365 contract."

"Great — that's actually a tailwind, not a blocker. Copilot drove organization-wide AI literacy for you, which means we can skip the 'what is AI' conversation and go straight to the workloads where model quality determines whether a project ships."

"Most of our enterprise customers run both. Copilot for ambient productivity inside Office; us for the high-stakes workflows — agent automation, voice experiences, customer-facing AI built on the API. I'd rather show you side-by-side on three of your real use cases than argue in the abstract."

03"The pricing is unpredictable — we can't budget for consumption."

"I hear that constantly, and it's a fair concern. Two ways we solve it. One: for seat-based products like ChatGPT Enterprise the pricing is fully predictable — per-seat, annual, no surprises. Two: for API workloads we do committed-use agreements with volume discounts, so you know your floor and your ceiling before you sign."

"And before any of that, we'll build a joint ROI model on one specific use case so you're not budgeting for ambiguity — you're budgeting against a quantified outcome."

04"We're going to wait until the technology stabilizes."

"I'd push back gently on the framing. The technology isn't going to 'stabilize' on a timeline that's useful to your competitors — they're not waiting. The question isn't whether to invest, it's whether you build organizational muscle now while the stakes are low or try to catch up when a competitor has a two-year head start on data, workflows, and talent."

"What I'd suggest: pick one bounded use case, run a 90-day pilot with clear success criteria, and either you've learned something cheaply or you've earned the case for a bigger move."

05"We're going to build this in-house."

"Some of our best customers thought the same thing — and many of them are still building, just on top of our platform instead of trying to replicate it. Two questions worth asking your team: how long would it take you to match the current frontier, and what happens when the frontier moves while you're building?"

"The build-vs-buy line for foundational capability has moved. I'd rather your engineers spend their time on what's truly proprietary to your business — not on rebuilding plumbing. Happy to share how peer companies decided where to draw that line."

06"How is this different from what we tried two years ago that didn't work?"

"Honest answer: a lot. Model quality is dramatically better, the tooling around evaluation and fine-tuning is real now, agentic and voice surfaces didn't exist, and the enterprise admin layer is mature. The category looks like the same word — AI — but the underlying capability is in a different decade."

"What I'd love is to look at exactly what didn't work last time. Half the time the failure was scoping or change-management, not the model — and getting that right is more valuable than any feature."

07"Our regulator hasn't blessed this yet."

"Then let's start where the regulator is comfortable. Internal productivity workloads — drafting, summarization, code review — are typically well within existing policy. We can sequence so that the customer-facing or regulated use cases come after you've built precedent and your compliance team has had a chance to develop the controls."

"I'll also connect you with our policy team and customers in your industry who've already navigated similar conversations with their regulators."

08"We need this to integrate with our existing stack — Salesforce, ServiceNow, Snowflake."

"Good — that's table stakes for us. We have first-party integrations and partner connectors across the major systems of record, and where we don't, our API surface and partner ecosystem cover the rest. Let me get your platform lead on with our SE so we can map the specific integration points to your reference architecture, not a generic one."

09"Our security team will never approve this in time for the quarter."

"Let's not let the timeline be the obstacle. We have SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001, and a security-review pack that pre-answers about 80% of the questions your team will ask. I'll get that to your CISO this week so they can start in parallel with the business case — not in series after it."

"If we shave even three weeks off security review, we're back inside the quarter window."

10"Why should we believe you over your CEO's last roadmap statement?"

"Fair pushback. I won't sell you a future I can't ship. What I will do is show you what's GA today, what's in beta with reference customers I can introduce you to, and what's on the public roadmap with documented timelines. Anything past that is a conversation, not a commitment — and I'll tell you the difference every time."

Tab 05 · API Expansion

From seats to platform.

The biggest accounts in this role aren't seat deals — they're API-native customers where our platform is embedded in their product. Spotting that arc early is the difference between a six-figure account and an eight-figure one.

Spotting an API-native customer

Strong signals

  • The product itself is software — not a service that uses software internally.
  • An engineering culture with a reputation for shipping; in-house ML or applied science already exists.
  • Their customers would notice (and pay for) AI-powered features directly.
  • Internal tools team is already wrapping our API in a private SDK.
  • Product roadmap explicitly references AI capabilities tied to user-facing outcomes.
  • A current vendor stack that includes embeddings, vector DBs, or model-routing layers.

Weak / disqualifying signals

  • "AI strategy" lives entirely in IT with no product representation.
  • No public-facing software product, or product is a thin wrapper around services.
  • Engineering leadership treats AI as a marketing topic.
  • Procurement-led conversations with no product or engineering presence.
  • Compliance posture so conservative that customer-facing AI is out of scope for years.

The expansion arc

STAGE 01

Internal productivity seats

ChatGPT Enterprise lands in one or two functions. Goal: prove value, build literacy, identify champions. Revenue is modest; signal value is high.

STAGE 02

API experimentation

Engineers from the customer's product team hit the API for prototypes. Often started without procurement involvement. My job: get visibility, offer SE help, prevent stall-out.

STAGE 03

Product-native embedding

An AI feature ships to the customer's customers, powered by us. Now we're inside their P&L story, not just their cost center. Pricing moves to committed use.

STAGE 04

Platform dependency

Multiple product lines depend on us. The conversation is now about latency, model cadence, and roadmap alignment. Multi-year, multi-million dollar commitment with co-marketed reference architectures.

Tab 07 · Killer Answers

Sixteen questions I should never fumble.

Concise, defensible, real. Each answer is something I can say in under 90 seconds and then defend with a follow-up if pressed.

01How would you sell to a CISO worried about data privacy?

I'd start by acknowledging that "privacy" usually means three different concerns — training-data leakage, residency, and audit. I'd address each on its merits: Enterprise data isn't used to train models by default, residency options exist where required, and the audit log gives the security team forensic visibility they likely don't get from their current SaaS stack.

Then I'd offer something concrete: a 30-minute working session between their security team and our trust team, with our SOC 2 and pen-test reports in advance. CISOs respect specifics, not slogans.

02Walk me through your biggest enterprise deal.

I'd pick a deal where I can name (a) the strategic premise, (b) one decision I made under pressure, (c) what I'd do differently. Sequence: discovery uncovered an unstated fear about a competitor's roadmap; I reframed the deal from a feature comparison to a strategic-bet conversation; built a champion in a function I wasn't initially calling on; found and neutralized a hidden detractor in InfoSec; and closed inside the customer's fiscal year by working their procurement timeline backward from signature.

What I'd do differently: get to the economic buyer two months earlier. The deal closed, but I burned weeks polishing decks for a mid-level audience.

03How do you think about land-and-expand?

Land for proof, not for revenue. The first deployment exists to generate one defensible internal case study with named owners and quantified outcomes. Without that, expansion is a cold start.

For expansion, I run two motions in parallel: sideways — taking the same playbook to adjacent functions before renewal — and upward — moving from seat deployment to the API once engineering and product see what their colleagues are doing.

04Why OpenAI over competitors?

Three reasons I'd defend in any room. One: model quality and release cadence at the frontier, with a track record of being first on capability that customers actually use — voice, agents, multi-modal. Two: the breadth of the platform — Enterprise, API, Codex, Realtime, fine-tuning — under one accountable counterparty. Three: the brand familiarity end-users already have, which collapses change-management cost on day one.

I won't claim we win every comparison; I will claim we're the partner most likely to still be on the frontier in 24 months.

05How would you handle a customer who wants to use Claude instead?

I'd respect the choice and ask why. If it's a specific capability they've evaluated, I want to learn from it. If it's safety posture, I'd talk about our enterprise controls and trust framework. If it's a hedge, I'd reframe — most serious enterprises run more than one model anyway; the question is which one is your platform of record.

I'd never disparage the competitor. I'd offer a side-by-side on three of their actual use cases and let the data do the work. If we lose on a use case, I'd rather know early.

06What's your forecasting discipline?

A deal isn't in my forecast unless I can name the economic buyer, the close mechanism, the paper process status, and a defensible exit criterion for each remaining stage. I'd rather sandbag a deal and over-deliver than sing a deal in and apologize at QBR. Forecasts are a form of public commitment to the rest of the company; I treat them that way.

07Tell me about a deal you lost.

I'd pick a real one and be specific. The shape of the answer: I lost because I didn't pressure-test my champion's political capital. He believed the deal would close; the CFO didn't see the ROI, and my champion didn't have the relationship to push back. I should have asked for a CFO meeting at week six, not week sixteen. Since then, I gate myself: no proposal goes out without a confirmed economic-buyer touchpoint within the prior 30 days.

08How do you handle a stalled deal?

First, I diagnose. Stalls usually mean either a missing stakeholder, an unaddressed objection, or an internal priority shift. I send a written "where we are, where we're stuck" email to the champion and ask them to react. The response — or lack of one — tells me which of the three it is.

Then I pick the smallest re-engagement that creates a forced moment: a working session, a peer-customer call, or, if needed, a hard ask: "should we pause and revisit Q3?" Sometimes the right deal is one I let go for now.

09How do you build a champion?

Champions aren't found, they're built. I look for someone whose career is improved by this deal succeeding — not someone who's just curious. I give them more than they can give me: market intel, peer introductions, narrative they can use internally. I make them look smart in front of their boss, in writing, repeatedly. By the time we get to procurement, they're not selling for me — they're selling for themselves.

10How do you sell to a CFO?

Numbers, not narrative. Every CFO I've sold to wants the same three artifacts: a quantified baseline, a defensible delta, and a payback window. I bring a joint ROI model — built with their FP&A team, not my marketing team — anchored on one auditable use case.

I also volunteer the risks. CFOs trust people who tell them what could go wrong before they have to ask. "Here's what would have to be true for this to not work" is the most underrated phrase in enterprise sales.

11What does your discovery process look like?

I run discovery in three layers. Surface — the publicly known business priorities, from earnings calls and 10-Ks. Operational — what's happening on the ground, learned from individual contributors and middle managers. Political — who has informal power, who's at risk, whose career is improved by change.

I take notes obsessively, share them back with the customer in writing, and let them correct me. The act of being corrected accelerates trust faster than any pitch.

12How do you keep a customer from churning?

Churn starts in month two of the deployment, not month eleven. The signals are usage flat-lining, the champion going quiet, and a new exec entering the picture without a relationship to me. I run a structured check-in cadence with both the champion and the user community, and I treat any quarter without a measurable usage win as a yellow flag.

For the renewal itself, I want a documented expansion case open at least two quarters in advance. Renewals shouldn't be conversations about price; they should be conversations about scope.

13How do you handle procurement?

I treat procurement as a stakeholder, not an obstacle. Their job is risk-adjusted savings; mine is to make them look like they did their job. I bring market benchmarks, an MSA pre-redlined to common enterprise asks, and a flexibility menu — term length, payment timing, service levels — so they have something to negotiate with that doesn't come out of the price.

Engaging procurement in week two is much cheaper than engaging them in week ten.

14What's the hardest part of selling frontier AI?

Pacing the customer. The technology is moving faster than most enterprise planning cycles. If I let the customer build a 12-month roadmap based on today's capability, I'll have to rewrite it twice before we sign. My job is to design deals that stay valuable through capability shifts — modular use cases, committed-use rather than feature-locked pricing, and quarterly steering committees so the customer expects the surface to evolve.

15Why this role, and why now?

Because the category is in the rare window where the technology, the buyer, and the budget are all moving at the same time. That window doesn't last. The companies that pick partners well in the next 18 months will compound advantage for a decade, and the ones that don't will spend the rest of their careers catching up.

I want to be the person an executive remembers as the one who got it right. That's a career-defining role for someone who likes both the strategy and the closing.

16What questions do you have for me?

Three I'd actually ask. One: how does the team think about the seat-vs-API tension inside a single account — is there one quota or two? Two: what's the bar I'd be measured on in the first 90 days, and is it relationship-building or pipeline? Three: tell me about a deal someone on the team lost recently, and what the team learned from it. The third one tells me more about the culture than any answer to the first two.

Mindset reminders

You are interviewing them too.

This is a peer conversation, not a beg. They want to be sure you're a good bet; you want to be sure the role is. Curiosity beats deference — every time.

Slow is smooth, smooth is fast.

Pause before answering. A two-second beat before you speak makes every sentence land harder. Talking fast is the most common nervousness tell — kill it deliberately.

Specific beats clever.

Names, numbers, dates, decisions. If you find yourself reaching for an abstraction, anchor back to a real customer story. Generality is the enemy.

Your opening 60 seconds

Beat 1 · 15 sec — Frame

"Thanks for the time today. Before I dive in, I want to tell you the lens I'm bringing to this conversation, because I think it's the lens you'd want from someone in this seat."

Beat 2 · 25 sec — Thesis

"I don't think AI is a point solution that lives inside IT. I think it's the operating substrate of every department in the next 24 months — and the partner that gets picked is the one with the best model and the most complete platform around it. That's the deal I want to be selling."

Beat 3 · 20 sec — Bridge

"I've spent my career closing strategic enterprise deals. What I want from this role is to do that at the only company where the product itself is the frontier. Happy to start anywhere you want — discovery, my biggest deal, or a hypothetical. Where would you like to begin?"

Tab 09 · What Not To Say

The unforced errors.

Each of these has cost someone a deal — or an offer. None are about content; all are about discipline. Read once, internalize, never look at again.

⛔ Don't

Trash competitors by name.

"Claude is a toy." "Gemini hallucinates constantly." Even if true, you sound insecure and the interviewer wonders how you'd talk about them in two years.

Instead: respect the competitor, then explain the conditions under which you win. Strength + specificity beats sneer + slogan.
⛔ Don't

Quote pricing you're not sure about.

If you make up a number — or worse, a ratio — and they spot it, you've handed them a reason to doubt every other claim you make in the conversation.

Instead: "I'd rather not anchor on a number I can't defend. The motion is per-seat for the productivity SKUs and committed-use for API; I can talk through the structure without quoting figures."
⛔ Don't

Claim features that don't exist yet.

Roadmap as fact is the fastest way to lose technical credibility — especially in a room with people who track the public roadmap better than you do.

Instead: "That's not GA today. It's been previewed publicly; I'd want to confirm the timeline before I commit to it in a customer conversation."
⛔ Don't

Use "AGI" in a sales context.

It signals hype, alarms compliance buyers, and dates you. Customers don't buy AGI; they buy outcomes their CFO can underwrite.

Instead: talk about the next 18 months of capability — agents, voice, multi-modal — in concrete, customer-facing terms.
⛔ Don't

Position AI as replacing jobs.

Even when a customer says it first, don't echo it. You've just made yourself a procurement problem and an HR problem in one sentence — and the next executive in the meeting is now distracted.

Instead: "What we tend to see is the work changing shape. Routine tasks compress; expert judgment expands. The customers who get this right reinvest the savings, not redistribute the headcount."
⛔ Don't

Promise outcomes you can't measure.

"You'll see a 40% productivity lift." Says who? Says when? Measured how? If the customer holds you to it, you'll be defending a number you invented.

Instead: "Peer customers in your industry are reporting outcomes in this range, and I'd rather build a joint baseline before I commit to a number for you."
⛔ Don't

Explain the technology when they asked about the business.

An executive asks "why now"; you answer "transformer architecture." You've just told them you don't know how to translate.

Instead: answer the business question in business language. Save the architecture for the SE meeting — or for the executive who asks for it explicitly.
⛔ Don't

Apologize for the price.

"I know it's expensive…" You've just told them it's too much. Now they're negotiating from a position you handed them.

Instead: anchor on value. "Here's what this saves, here's what it accelerates, here's what's at stake if you wait. The price is the price; let's talk about whether the case is real."
⛔ Don't

Speak negatively about prior employers.

The interviewer is mentally substituting "OpenAI" for the name of the company you're criticizing. Every story should be told as something you learned, not something they did wrong.

Instead: "I'm grateful for what I learned there. Here's what made me ready for a different stage of company."
⛔ Don't

Confuse activity with progress.

"I had 14 meetings last quarter." That's a calendar, not a forecast. The interviewer wants to know what moved.

Instead: name the deal, the stage, the decision you forced, and the outcome. "I drove a CFO meeting in week six that re-anchored the deal on payback. Closed in 11 weeks instead of the usual 16."
⛔ Don't

Hide behind "I'd loop in my SE."

You can do that twice in an interview. The third time, the interviewer concludes you can't carry a technical conversation without help. This is an IC role — show you can hold the room.

Instead: answer at the level you can defend, then say where you'd bring the SE in for depth. "I can speak to model selection and latency trade-offs; for retrieval architecture I'd want to bring in our SE so we get it right."
⛔ Don't

Use buzzwords without a referent.

"Synergistic," "leverage" as a verb in a vague sentence, "transformational." If you can't replace the word with a concrete noun, don't use it.

Instead: say what you actually mean. "We replaced 3 of their 5 vendor contracts with one platform" beats "transformational consolidation."

Section 2

5 Questions They'll Likely Ask

  • 1
    "What have you actually built with AI?" Lead with Gary. Multi-agent dispatch pipeline, 110+ MCP tools, autonomous project execution with stage gates. Emphasize that this is a production system you use daily, not a weekend project. Mention the model routing layer (different models for different cost/quality tradeoffs) as evidence of real operational thinking.
  • 2
    "How do you think about agentic systems vs. traditional automation?" Agents make decisions about what to do next based on context. Traditional automation follows fixed rules. The hard part is not the inference call; it is the orchestration layer: guardrails, cost caps, fallback routing, and knowing when to escalate to a human. That is what you built in Gary's dispatch system.
  • 3
    "Why are you interested in OpenAI specifically?" Be honest and precise. OpenAI has the strongest enterprise distribution motion, the largest developer ecosystem, and is investing heavily in enterprise sales infrastructure. If the conversation is about agentic systems, note that you have direct experience building on multi-model architectures and understand what enterprise buyers actually need from an AI platform vendor.
  • 4
    "What's your view on where enterprise AI adoption is stuck?" Enterprises are stuck at the pilot-to-production gap. The demo is easy. Reorganizing a workflow around model outputs is hard. The market is overpricing demos and underpricing deployment. The AE who can walk a customer from "cool demo" to "we changed how the team works" is the one who closes real deals.
  • 5
    "What verticals do you know well?" Industrial/manufacturing (Cisco IoT Americas, Nokia Private 5G), sports & entertainment (Cisco S&E — NFL, NBA, NHL venues), and enterprise campus infrastructure. For OpenAI's push into finance, biotech, and industrial verticals, your industrial experience is directly relevant.
Section 3

5 Questions You Should Ask

  • 1
    "Where is the enterprise team seeing the most friction between what the model can do and what the customer can actually deploy?" Shows you understand the pilot-to-production gap from firsthand experience. Opens a conversation about real customer pain, not hypothetical capability.
  • 2
    "How does the enterprise team think about competing with customers building internally on open-weight models versus selling them OpenAI's managed platform?" Demonstrates you understand the real competitive landscape. Not "who's your competitor?" — it is the customer's own engineering team. Shows strategic depth.
  • 3
    "What does the ideal AE look like for the industrial and manufacturing segment? Is the team structured by vertical or by account size?" Practical question that signals you are thinking about where you would fit and how to be effective, not just whether you can get the job.
  • 4
    "How is OpenAI thinking about the agentic layer — is it primarily an API play, or are you building opinionated orchestration frameworks that enterprises can deploy?" You have direct experience building orchestration (Gary's dispatch pipeline). This positions you to have a substantive technical conversation, not a surface-level one.
  • 5
    "What's the relationship between the research team's pace of new capability releases and the enterprise team's ability to keep customers current?" This is a real operational challenge at every frontier lab. Shows you understand the internal tension and have thought about how a sales org navigates it.
Section 4

Your 60-Second Narrative

The Setup

I spent 15 years in enterprise technology sales — Cisco and Nokia — selling complex infrastructure into Fortune 500s. Director of IoT Americas, CTO of Sports & Entertainment, Head of Enterprise Campus Edge. I know how large organizations buy technology and how long it takes to earn their trust.

The Turn

About a year ago, I started building my own AI operating system. Not as a product to sell — as a tool to run my own work. I wanted to understand what agentic AI actually feels like in production, not in a demo. That system now has over 110 tools, multi-agent dispatch, autonomous execution with guardrails, and cost-controlled model routing across multiple providers.

But the real sale is helping customers reorganize workflows around model outputs. That requires someone who has done it — not just talked about it. That's the perspective I would bring.

Tip: Deliver this conversationally, not as a monologue. Pause after "the turn" — they will likely ask follow-up questions about what you built. Let them pull the details out of you.

Section 5

What to Avoid Saying

  • 1
    Don't name-drop other labs' models favorably You built Gary on Claude and other providers. That's fine as personal context, but don't lead with "I built on Anthropic's stack" in an OpenAI conversation. If asked directly, be honest and frame it as model-agnostic architecture — you route to the best model for each task, which is exactly what enterprises do.
  • 2
    Don't oversell Gary as a product Gary is a proof-of-concept that demonstrates your technical depth. It is not a company, not a product with users, and not a competitor. Frame it as: "I built this to understand the problem space from the inside."
  • 3
    Don't critique OpenAI's safety posture You may have opinions about different labs' approaches to safety. Keep them to yourself in this conversation. If safety comes up, pivot to what enterprise buyers care about: data privacy, SOC 2, deployment controls. Stay practical, not philosophical.
  • 4
    Don't talk about cost as a differentiator Avoid framing like "I run Gary for $15/week" or comparing model pricing. Enterprise buyers don't optimize for pennies per token — they optimize for deployment risk and vendor trust. Keep cost discussions at the strategic level: "I understand the economics of running inference at scale."
  • 5
    Don't volunteer opinions on OpenAI's competitive position Market share numbers, comparisons to Google or Anthropic, takes on pricing strategy — none of this helps you. If they bring it up, listen and ask follow-up questions. Let them tell you how they see the market. You learn more and avoid saying something that lands wrong.

"When in doubt, ask a question instead of making a statement. The person with the questions controls the conversation."
